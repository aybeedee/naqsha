Methodology

How the flood map works

The map shows a test storm: 100 mm of rain over two hours in Central Lahore and Gulberg–Liberty. It does not show today's forecast.

1. Three versions of the ground

We use three public elevation maps: Copernicus, FABDEM and an SRTM-family map. Each is divided into cells roughly 29 metres wide. In flat Lahore, small height differences between these maps can change where water goes. FABDEM comes from Copernicus, so the three maps are not independent measurements.

2. Run the flood model

We run a flood model called SFINCS once for each elevation map, using the same rain. We save the water depth every ten minutes during the storm and for two hours afterward. Every run uses the same assumed surface loss (5 mm per hour), ground roughness (0.06) and open edges where water can leave. These are simplifying assumptions, not measurements of Lahore's drains.

3. Read the result

At each place and time, the main view shows the middle water depth from the three runs. “Whole event” shows the highest saved depth at each place; those peaks may happen at different times. “Model agreement” counts how many runs passed 10 cm; it does not tell you the chance of flooding.

We check mapped roads against the simulated water to show potentially affected segments. Buildings and place names help you find your way around; buildings do not block water in the model. The 3D water is drawn taller so it is easier to see.

Where the map stops

The grid cannot show kerbs, underpasses or individual entrances. We do not have detailed ground and drainage surveys, or local flood observations to check the results against. A flagged road may still be passable; an unflagged one may not be. This is an experiment, not a street-level warning or a live forecast.
