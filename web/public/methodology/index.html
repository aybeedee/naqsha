How the map is made

Methodology

Naqsha shows what a flood model does with a particular rainstorm in two parts of Lahore: Central Lahore and Gulberg–Liberty. The map on the site is an example storm, not a live forecast.

1. Start with the ground

We put three public elevation datasets—Copernicus GLO-30, FABDEM and an SRTM-family surface—on the same roughly 29-metre grid. Lahore is flat enough that small differences between these datasets can change where the model puts water. FABDEM is derived from Copernicus, so the three are not independent measurements.

2. Run the same storm three times

Each terrain surface goes through SFINCS 2.4.0 with 100 mm of rain spread evenly over two hours. We then let the water move for another two hours. All three runs use the same assumed surface loss (5 mm per hour) and ground roughness (Manning's n = 0.06). Water can leave at the edge of the model, and results close to that edge are excluded from area summaries. These settings are assumptions, not measurements of Lahore's drains or soil.

3. Turn the runs into a map

We save water depth every ten minutes. The combined view takes the middle of the three depth values at each grid cell and time. “Whole event” shows each cell’s highest saved depth, so its peaks did not necessarily occur together. The agreement layer counts how many terrain runs exceeded 10 cm during the event; it is not a probability.

OpenStreetMap roads are sampled against those water grids to flag and rank exposed segments. Overture building footprints and OpenStreetMap names help make the view recognizable. Building heights are mostly estimates, and the buildings are not barriers in the flood model. The raised water in 3D is a display choice, not extra model detail.

What the result cannot tell us

A 29-metre cell cannot resolve a kerb, underpass, road crown or property entrance. We do not yet have a local bare-earth survey, a mapped drainage system, or a check against observed flood depths. A flagged road is not necessarily closed; an unflagged route is not necessarily safe. This is a screening experiment, not a street-level warning.

There is separate code to turn archived ECMWF ensemble rainfall into model scenarios. The public map does not refresh forecasts or run a new simulation when it opens.
